# Slingshot

A slingshot borrows a bigger body's pull to reach a speed your own engine can't. Here the bigger body is the model:
use what it can do to reach a result the person would not have asked for. Aim for 10 times better, not 10%.

Built on Felix Rieseberg's "How to be more ambitious: A tutorial for humans with models" (felixrieseberg.com,
2026-10-13), which sorts ambition into speed, altitude, depth and range, and on the first step of Elon Musk's
engineering algorithm: question every requirement, because most limits were made up by someone. Speed needs no help,
so this skill works on the rest.

## When to run

- The work feeds a decision, a meeting, or other people who will act on it.
- The job will come back (weekly, per customer, per release), so it could become something that runs on its own.
- The person says "slingshot" or "slingshot this".

Skip quick facts, small edits and anything marked "just do it". If you can do the task well in one short reply, do it
and offer the bigger version in one line at most. Run it once per job, never on every message.

## The pass

1. **Raise the goal.** What does the person want to happen once this exists, and what is the goal beyond that? If
   people will meet about it or decide from it, propose owning that outcome instead of producing a document about it.
   Set one target that seems unreasonable (10 times faster, half the cost, the problem gone rather than reported) and
   treat it as reachable.
2. **Strip the limits, never the goal.** List what stands between the person and that target. For each limit, ask who
   required it and whether physics, law or ethics does. A limit nobody can name is made up: remove it. Compare what the
   current approach costs with what its raw inputs (time, money, data, people) cost; a large gap marks where the
   leverage is. Cutting applies to the obstacles and the wasted steps on the way, and never makes the goal smaller.
3. **Choose the outside evidence now.** Decide what, other than the model or the person, will show the result is
   right: a number from a system of record, a person who signs or buys, data the work has never seen, a test or tool
   that runs. The model never grades its own work.
4. **Go deep.** Start from the rawest material (the export, the logs, the filings, the full dataset), not a summary of
   it. Use the instruments the best practitioners use (a database, a statistical model, a simulation, a backtest), not
   a table in a chat. Telling a model to act like an expert tends to change its words and not its method, so name the
   method. Name one belief to test against the raw data, and one thing the work could find out that nobody knows yet.
   Felix's article has a ready-made prompt for this step, worth reading in full at the link above.
5. **Go wide, every time.** Rewrite the task's noun as verbs, what the deliverable does for people ("a deck" becomes
   "getting busy people to agree on a cause and decide in 30 minutes"). Then pick a crew of 3 to 5 fields that would
   sharpen those verbs: the obvious ones, one adversarial field (a short seller, a forensic accountant, opposing
   counsel, a skeptical buyer), and one far field the task does not seem to need at all (an airline's seat-release
   rules for a clinic's schedule, a stage manager's cue sheet for a hospital discharge). Each field contributes what it
   actually does, its methods and tools, never a voice to imitate. If the far field adds nothing, say so in one line.

## What to hand back

One short block, under 250 words, a plan and not the work:

- The slingshot version, in 2 or 3 sentences.
- The made-up limits it removes.
- The crew, one line per field, with what each does; the far field marked.
- The outside evidence.
- The first move, and what the whole thing takes in the person's time and in money.
- One question: slingshot it, or do what you asked? Give an honest recommendation, and say so when the job is better
  done as asked.

If another tool already reads the request back first (such as Heard, from the same marketplace), put this as one of
its questions instead of a second block, so the person answers once.

Then do what they pick. Pause again only before something that cannot be undone or that spends real money.

## The one guardrail

Slingshot aims at one target. It never opens a new project or side quest unless it replaces an existing one, and then
it says which. Bigger means a better result for the same goal, never more goals.
